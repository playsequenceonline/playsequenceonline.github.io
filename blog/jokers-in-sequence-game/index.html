Jokers in Sequence: are they used, and what are they for?

Published November 17, 2026 · Chipline

No, jokers are not used in standard Sequence. The game is played with two regular 52-card decks (104 cards), and none of the 100 board spaces shows a joker, so any jokers in your box are simply set aside. The wild-card job belongs to the two-eyed jacks, while one-eyed jacks remove an opponent's chip.

Key takeaways

  • Official Sequence rules do not use jokers. Take them out before you shuffle.
  • The deck is two standard decks without jokers: 104 cards, including 8 jacks.
  • The 10x10 board has 96 card spaces plus 4 free corners. There is no joker space.
  • Two-eyed jacks are the real "jokers" of Sequence: they are wild and go on any open space.
  • One-eyed jacks remove an opponent's chip (but not one that is part of a finished sequence).
  • Jokers make good house-rule cards or stand-ins for a lost card, if everyone agrees first.

Are jokers used in Sequence?

Not in the standard game. A Sequence deck is made of two ordinary packs of playing cards, and the game uses the 52 regular cards from each pack. Jokers have no matching space on the board, so if you drew one you could not play it. That is why the usual instruction is to leave them in the box.

This surprises a lot of first-time players, because the jokers are right there in the card packs. Seeing them, people often assume the jokers must be wild. They are not. In Sequence, the wild cards are certain jacks, which we cover below.

Why does Sequence come with jokers, then?

The simple reason is that standard playing cards are printed and packed with jokers. The game uses ordinary decks, so the jokers come along for the ride. They are not a hidden rule or a missing expansion.

They can still be handy. Many families keep them as spares: if a card goes missing, a joker can stand in for it (more on that below). Others use them for house rules once they know the normal game well.

So which card works like a joker in Sequence?

The two-eyed jack is Sequence's joker. It is wild, so you can put your chip on any open space on the board, which makes it the most powerful card in the game.

The one-eyed jack is the opposite: instead of adding a chip, it takes one away. You can remove any single chip belonging to an opponent, as long as that chip is not already part of a completed sequence. You cannot place your own chip on the same turn.

Telling them apart is easy once you look closely: the jack of hearts and jack of spades are drawn in profile, so you see one eye. The jack of diamonds and jack of clubs face forward with two eyes. For the full breakdown, see one-eyed vs two-eyed jacks in Sequence.

What should you do if a joker gets shuffled in?

It happens, especially with a new box. If someone draws a joker mid-game, the fair fix is simple:

  1. Show it and set it aside. Put the joker back in the box so it does not come round again.
  2. Draw a replacement. The player takes a new card from the draw pile so their hand is the right size.
  3. Carry on. No turn is lost. If the joker was dealt at the very start, just swap it for the next card.

Better still, fan through the deck and pull out the jokers before the first deal. It takes ten seconds and avoids the debate.

What are some popular house rules for jokers?

None of these are official, and every table plays them a little differently. If you want to try one, agree on it before the first card is dealt. Here are the most common ideas players use:

  1. Extra wild cards. Shuffle in two jokers that act like two-eyed jacks. More wild cards mean faster games and more comebacks.
  2. Extra removers. Jokers act like one-eyed jacks. This makes the game more defensive and usually longer.
  3. Lost-card stand-in. If a card from the deck is missing, mark a joker (or tape a note to it) and let it count as that card. This keeps all 96 board spaces reachable.
  4. Kids' helper card. Give a younger player one joker they can use as a wild once per game, as a gentle handicap for mixed-age family nights.

Should you add jokers to your game?

For a first game, no. Play the standard rules so everyone learns how jacks, corners and dead cards really work. The game is already well balanced: with 8 jacks in 104 cards, wild cards and removers show up often enough to create swings without making the board feel random.

Once your group knows the game, jokers are a fun way to change the pace. Extra wilds suit a loud party table where people want quick rounds. If your games tend to drag, though, avoid extra removers, which give everyone more ways to break up lines. Whatever you choose, the win condition stays the same: a sequence is five chips in a row, and two players or two teams need two sequences to win, while three teams need just one.
