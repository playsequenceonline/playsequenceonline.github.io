Sequence game strategy: 12 tips to win more often

The best Sequence strategy in one line: place chips that help two lines at once, use the corners, start blocking when an opponent has three in a row, and keep your jacks for the move that wins or saves the game.

Sequence looks like a luck game, but the same players tend to win. These tips work for the boxed board game and for Chipline, our free online version. New to the game? Read the rules of Sequence first.

Building lines

1. Make every chip work for two lines

A chip in the middle of nowhere helps one possible line. A chip where a row and a diagonal cross helps two. When you can choose between two spaces, pick the one that sits next to more of your own chips, in more directions.

The goal is a double threat: two different spaces that would each finish a line. Your opponent can only cover one of them on their turn.

2. Use the corners: they are a free chip

The four corner stars count as a chip for every side. A line that runs through a corner needs only four of your chips instead of five. Lines along the edges and the two long diagonals can use them, so cards for spaces near a corner are worth more than they look.

3. Prefer the middle early on

A space in the middle of the board can be part of lines in every direction. A space on the edge has fewer options. Early in the game, when nothing is threatened yet, lean towards central spaces, and towards corners as in tip 2.

4. With two teams, plan your second line from the start

When there are two sides, you need two lines to win, and your second line may reuse one chip from your first. So build your first line where a second one can cross it. A finished line is also safe: chips in a completed sequence can never be removed.

Blocking

5. Block at three, not at four

Two chips in a row are not a danger yet. Three in a row with open space on both ends is: one more chip gives your opponent two ways to finish, and you can only stop one. If you hold the card for either end of an open three, playing it is usually better than adding to your own scattered chips.

6. The best block also builds

Before blocking, check both spaces your card could go on. Often one of the blocking spaces also sits next to your own chips. A chip that blocks and builds at once is the strongest ordinary move in the game.

7. Count what can still hurt you

Every card has exactly two copies in the deck. If an opponent needs one specific space, they need one of two cards, or a two-eyed jack. If you have seen both copies played, or you hold one yourself, that space is far less urgent. There are only four two-eyed jacks and four one-eyed jacks in the whole deck, so keep a rough count of the ones already played.

Using your jacks

8. Two-eyed jacks: hold them for the fifth chip

A two-eyed jack (diamonds or clubs) goes on any open space. That makes it the one card that can finish any line of four, or take the single space an opponent needs. Playing it early for an ordinary chip throws that away. Keep it until it wins a line or stops one.

9. One-eyed jacks: break a four, or clear your own path

A one-eyed jack (spades or hearts) removes one opponent chip that is not part of a finished line. Its two best uses are taking a chip out of the middle of an opponent's four in a row, and removing the one chip that blocks your own line. Taking a chip from the middle of a line hurts more than taking one from the end, because the gap is harder to refill.

It cannot touch a completed sequence, so if an opponent is one chip from finishing, remove a chip before the line is complete, not after.

Managing your hand

10. Swap dead cards straight away

If both spaces for a card are covered, the card is dead. Swap it at the start of your turn for a new one: it costs nothing and you still take your normal turn. A dead card left in your hand is one fewer real choice every turn.

11. Keep the cards that give you choices

If you hold both copies of a card, those two spaces are yours whenever you want them, so there is no hurry to play them. Play the cards that might be taken from you first: spaces an opponent could also want. Save the guaranteed ones for when they finish a line.

Team play

12. Finish your partner's lines, and never block them

Teammates cannot talk about their cards, so the board is your only message. When your partner places a chip, ask what line they are starting and add to it if you can. Before every move, check that your chip is not sitting on a space your own team needed open for a different line.

Questions

What is the best strategy in Sequence?

Build chips that work for two lines at once, use the corners, block opponents as soon as they have three in a row with room to grow, and save your jacks for the moments that win or save the game.

When should I use a two-eyed jack?

Hold it until it completes one of your lines or fills the one space an opponent needs for a fifth chip. Spending a wild jack on an ordinary move early in the game is the most common mistake.

When should I use a one-eyed jack?

Use it to remove a chip from an opponent's line of four, or to clear the single chip blocking your own line. It cannot remove a chip from a finished sequence.

Should I block or build?

Build while opponents have two or fewer in a row. Start blocking at three in a row with open space on both sides, and always block four in a row if you can.

Are the corners good in Sequence?

Yes. A corner counts as a chip for every team, so a line through a corner needs only four of your chips. Lines along the edges and the long diagonals can use them.

Is there a first-player advantage in Sequence?

Going first gives a small head start, but the cards you draw matter far more. Over a few games it evens out, which is why many groups rotate who starts.
